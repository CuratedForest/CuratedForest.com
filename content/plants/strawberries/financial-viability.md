There are a large number of factors that influence the financial viability of an idea. What's outlined here are the broad signals pointing to there being opportunity in strawberries. They are indicators, but the ideas still need to be validated. Obviously the best way to validate financial viability is with a proven history of profitability, but before that can happen, a business plan must show the possibility first. 

# Approach
Over the last decade, I've explored the CEA (Controlled Environment Agriculture: greenhouses & indoor) space, as well as the tech and startup worlds. I've watched as the CEA bubble expanded and more recently have watched it pop. What these experiences have taught me is that so much of our tech world isn't built on profits, but are built on the _promise_ of profits at _scale_. 

In CEA that's often looks like not just a promising business plan, but a small scale facility proving you can grow (but not necessarily profitability), a major technical lift, and a lot of labor, both manual to compensate for the failures in the technical components, as well as technical people to build and maintain the robots. The thing is, all this plus the electrical costs for lights and HVAC ends up being expensive, and it's competing with field and greenhouse crops whose lighting is provided for free and don't have HUGE capital expenditures to pay off. 

On top of that, once you do get the unit economics to a competitive point on paper, you're still competing with free light, so the margins are slim. If you can find the margins, then the play is to scale up until those margins get you to company wide profits. This is usually when the big money flows in as it's needed for the scale up.

If it all works and the unit economics are realized, great. But that's rarely how it goes, both with technical projects, as well as scaling a company. It takes time to sort out the details, processes, and failed assumptions. In the meantime the holes are plugged with money. These can be HUGE inefficiencies. Seriously!

What ultimately popped the CEA bubble was a big restriction in new investment money coming into the companies. No longer can you show a promising business plan, and a growing facility and get funded. Now, you have to be at least in reach of profitability to have any chance of more investments. Without the additional cash, we've seen many companies go through bankruptcy, if not under entirely.

It's hard to guard a company against a recession, especially in it's early stages. But the mistake being made is the scale at which we validate the unit economics.

## The Challenge
My tweak is to validate unit economics at a small scale before scaling up. It's harder to engineer a system that can be profitable at a smaller scale, especially because you're trying to automate or remove processes with fewer resources. But if I keep working at it until that's possible, the result _will_ be a better engineered, efficient, and competitive process. 

### Patience
The trick to this approach is patience, having a plan, and a target. We're at the birth of the CEA industry, and have to admit, for most crops, an indoor grow isn't even close to financially viable. But for a select few, with the right approach, it might be doable today. With that said, it might not be! Fortunately the world is constantly changing and I'm confident (for better or worse) that in my lifetime that scale will tip. If my approach to CEA truly can't be done profitability today, then my goal is to identify what could change to shift that (electric rates, LED prices, food shortages, etc), and being ready to pull the trigger on scaling when those conditions are met.

# The Payoff
Taking this kind of approach has a couple main payoffs.
- The biggest in that it's FAR less painful to learn lessons at a small scale and then go big than learn them at the large scale and have to pivot or redesign.
- Designing for profitability and a smaller scale will inevitability lead to more profits at a larger scale. 
- The company will have a more sound financial base and thus will weather hardships better and need fewer outside investors.

# Positive Signals
A great idea, too soon, goes unrealized. The world has to be ready, for an idea to really take off. These are the signs that make me think the world is ready for CEA strawberries.
## Poor Quality in Market (means we can charge premium)
Currently in the US, over 50% of our US grown grocery store strawberries come from 2 valleys in CA. The climate in these places is crazy ideal for growing strawberries specifically, and with transplanting and plasticulture, they grow prolifically. Yet so often their experience reminds me of Styrofoam! This is because they're grown for color, disease resistance, and shelf-life before flavor. By growing indoors and locally, we can relax the disease and shelf-life requirements in favor of flavor. By prioritizing flavor, we can market it as a premium product, and attach a premium price point. Oishii's designer berries are doing a great job proving that customers will pay high prices for a quality berry. Seriously! $2.5 - $3 **a berry**.

### Oishii
Oishii is a company grown out of the Japanese fresh fruit culture. They have a deep history of growing and valuing impeccable and delicious fruit. We don't label our strawberries with their variety, but in some Japan markets, not only do they list the variety, but the sweetness (in brix %) too! They are one of the few CEA companies that haven't been through any financial hardships or downsizing. A great example that: 
1) There is the possibility of profitability in CEA.
2) It takes a company that executes well.

## Plant Needs Match CEA
There are a couple of features of strawberry plants that fit CEA particularly well.
- The plants are compact. 
	- Important for being able to stack!
	- Not much of the plant's of energy goes to growing leafs.
- The time from planting to harvest is weeks.
	- Much longer than this and electricity prices eat you.
- Lower light needs.
	- Needs only ~1/4 the strength (PPFD/s) given to tomato plants.
- They go for a premium price.
	- Critical to pay for a month or more of lighting + HVAC
- Strawberry plants are more prone to suffer from a lack of O2 than other plants.
	- Aeroponics does a better job of providing O2 than other approaches.
- Strawberries are disease prone. 
	- Growing indoors helps significantly with the pest and disease load.
- Strawberries historically have a reputation for being pesticide heavy (part of the "dirty dozen").
	- Growing indoors means these pesticides aren't needed **at all**.

## People > Compensation 
One of the things corporate culture is most blind to is the impact employee happiness and retention has on output. Over and over again studies show that happy and engaged employees not only show up more and have a longer ten year, but they also do better work. There are a couple of components that often aren't considered. 

The first is the negative impact turnover has on potential output hours. You can always find replacements, true, but rarely will they come in being able to do a comparable job to who has left. So you're out on time while finding a replacement, and you're still losing hours every day while the new person gets up to speed. Not only that, but to get interviewed and trained also takes time from others in the company. 

The second is the quality of work. A stressed employee (because of a lack of time or money) is at best distracted, could be an efficiency drag, and at worst a danger. Take away this stress and now at worst they'll have time to focus at work, some may also not just find improvement to the processes, and at best do the emotional work of getting buy in and actually changing things.

### Competitive Advantage
Regardless of small or large scale, the key to profitability is having a team that works efficiently together, contributes to innovations in the processes, and maintains a high standard of quality in the products. The ability to do that directly translates to margins and competitiveness. So by treating employees like people, we're best setting the company, and the people that make it, up for success.

## Renewable Electricity 
One of the biggest disadvantages to growing indoors in the cost of electricity. I roughly account for 1/3 of a farm's expenses going to electricity alone. It's on par with labor. As more renewable and new energy technologies come online, we could see a drop in electricity prices... You know, once the data centers get their fill. This is a place where patience is helpful; energy prices very wildly across a nation and it may be important to locate the first farm in a strong energy market.

# Market Risks
The CEA space is currently a risky one. We've seen the majority of the big names in the field go under or otherwise shrink due to financial hardships and poor execution. Convincing investors that it's a good place to put their money is hard. Regardless, it is that risk, that struggle, that tells me there is value in this direction.
